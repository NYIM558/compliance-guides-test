Renters' Rights Act 2025: What Landlords and Letting Agents Must Do Before June 2026

By Hassan Published on 23 May 2026

Mandatory documentation, council enforcement powers, and penalties up to £40,000 — here's your complete compliance guide

The Renters' Rights Act 2025 came into force on 1 May 2026, and the clock is already ticking. Landlords and letting agents in England now face legally mandated documentation requirements, backed by substantial financial penalties and new investigatory powers for local councils. Whether you manage one property or one thousand, understanding these changes is essential.

This guide explains exactly what you need to do, by when, and what happens if you don't.

What Is the Renters' Rights Act 2025 and Why Does It Matter?

The Renters' Rights Act 2025 overhauls private rented sector regulation in England. Its centrepiece is a new mandatory Written Statement of Terms — a prescribed document that must be provided to every tenant, containing specific information defined by the Assured Tenancies (Private Rented Sector) (Written Statement of Terms etc and Information Sheet) (England) Regulations 2026.

This isn't just an upgraded tenancy agreement. It's a legally prescribed document with defined content requirements, and failure to provide it correctly can result in a £7,000 penalty per breach.

The Mandatory Written Statement of Terms (Section 16D)

Who is responsible?

Both the landlord and any letting agent or contractor responsible for compliance have separate, independent duties. If you manage a property on behalf of a landlord, you cannot rely on the landlord to cover your compliance obligations.

When must it be provided?

For new tenancies from 1 May 2026: before the tenancy is entered into. For tenancies that become assured at a later date: within 28 days of that date.

What format is acceptable?

Electronic delivery with e-signatures is permitted. The document does not need to be titled "tenancy agreement" — but it must contain all prescribed terms.

What Must the Written Statement Include?

Basic tenancy information

  • Full names of all landlords and tenants (including joint parties)
  • Full property address
  • Date the tenant is first entitled to possession
  • An address in England or Wales where the landlord can be served with notices

Financial terms

  • Rent amount and the date it falls due (rent periods cannot exceed monthly)
  • How rent increases will operate — this must reference the statutory Section 13 process
  • Details of any bills the tenant pays to the landlord
  • Tenancy deposit amount (where Housing Act 2004 deposit rules apply)

Tenant rights and possession process

  • Minimum notice period the tenant must give to end the tenancy
  • Clear statement that the landlord can only recover possession through a court order
  • Applicable notice periods for any relevant possession grounds

Safety and property standards

  • Confirmation the property is fit for human habitation
  • Landlord's repairing obligations under Section 11 (where applicable)
  • Electrical safety: inspections required every 5 years, report provided to tenant
  • Gas safety duties (where gas fittings are installed)

Specific tenant rights

  • Right to request disability-related improvements (consent cannot be unreasonably withheld)
  • Right to request a pet (consent cannot be unreasonably refused)
  • For supported accommodation: explanation of why the property qualifies

Critical: The Possession Grounds "Prior Notice" Requirement

This is one of the most important — and easily missed — elements of the new rules.

If a landlord may ever wish to use any of the "prior notice" possession grounds (Grounds 1B, 2ZA–2ZD, 4, 5–5H, 6A, or 18 under Schedule 2), they must state this intention in the written statement from day one.

If this statement is not included at the outset, those grounds cannot be relied upon later. You cannot add it retrospectively.

For student landlords, the same principle applies to Ground 4A (student churn ground) — the written statement must include the relevant student ground statement or it is unavailable.

Transitional Rules: What About Existing Tenancies?

The Act commenced on 1 May 2026, but most existing tenancies will not need a full new Section 16D document. Here is how the rules break down:

Tenancies wholly or partly in writing (this includes any signed agreement, renewal letter, email chain, portal record, or written variation):

  • No new written statement required
  • Must receive the prescribed Information Sheet 2026 explaining the Renters' Rights Act changes
  • Deadline: 31 May 2026

Tenancies that are wholly oral (no written element exists at all):

  • Must receive the full Section 16D written statement
  • Deadline: 31 May 2026

Where a letting agent manages the property, both the landlord and the agent have a duty to provide the relevant document. This is a dual obligation — neither party can assume the other has covered it.

Council Enforcement Powers: What Investigators Can Now Do

Local housing authorities have been given significantly expanded investigatory powers under Part 4 of the Act. These include:

  • Power to require information from any person (Section 115)
  • Power to enter business premises, with or without a warrant in certain circumstances
  • Power to require production of documents (Section 122)
  • Power to seize documents as evidence (Section 123)

These powers are modelled on consumer protection enforcement legislation and give councils the tools to pursue suspected breaches systematically, including accessing letting agent offices and demanding records.

Financial Penalties: How Much Could You Be Fined?

The penalty regime is set out in Schedule 5 of the Act and follows a structured process:

  • Notice of Intent — the council serves notice proposing a penalty (must be within 6 months of having sufficient evidence)
  • 28-day representation period — the landlord or agent can submit written representations
  • Final Notice — the council decides whether to impose the penalty and sets the amount
  • Payment deadline — 28 days from the final notice
  • Appeal route — to the First-tier Tribunal

Maximum penalties:

  • £7,000 for regulatory breaches, including failure to provide a compliant written statement under Section 16D
  • £40,000 for certain offences under Part 2 of the Act

Additional points to note: unpaid penalties are recoverable as a debt through the county court; councils must follow Secretary of State guidance when setting penalty amounts; if a breach continues for more than 28 days after a final notice, further penalties can be issued; and no penalty can be issued where criminal proceedings for the same conduct are ongoing or concluded.

Practical Compliance Checklist

For new tenancies from 1 May 2026:

  • Issue the written statement before the tenancy commences — not on the day, before
  • Verify it contains all prescribed terms and information
  • Include the possession ground "wish" statement where relevant to your circumstances
  • Retain evidence of delivery: email timestamps, e-signature records, read receipts

For existing tenancies — action required by 31 May 2026:

  • Audit your portfolio: identify which tenancies are wholly oral vs. partly or wholly written
  • For written/partly written tenancies: serve the 2026 Information Sheet
  • For wholly oral tenancies: serve the full Section 16D written statement
  • Document what was sent, to whom, when, and how

Record-keeping:

  • Retain complete signed copies or full PDFs with audit trails
  • Electronic signatures are particularly useful as they generate timestamped evidence of receipt prior to tenancy commencement
  • Both landlord and agent should hold their own copies

Avoid unfair terms: The Consumer Rights Act 2015 continues to apply alongside the new Act. Clauses that require professional cleaning regardless of property condition, transfer landlord repair duties to tenants, or permit unlimited landlord access without notice (outside genuine emergencies) remain unenforceable and should be removed from all templates.

Summary: What Agents and Landlords Need to Do Now

The Renters' Rights Act 2025 is not a gradual transition — the core obligations are already live. The most common compliance failures will be:

  • Missing the 31 May 2026 deadline for existing tenancies
  • Omitting the possession ground statement from new tenancy documents
  • Agents assuming the landlord has covered their separate obligations

The practical steps are straightforward: update your tenancy templates, train your team, serve the required documents on existing tenants before the end of May, and keep thorough records. Councils now have the powers and the financial incentives to enforce — penalties of up to £7,000 per breach are not trivial, and the investigatory powers make it harder to claim ignorance.
