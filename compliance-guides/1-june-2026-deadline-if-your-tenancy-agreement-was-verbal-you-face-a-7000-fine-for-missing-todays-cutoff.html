1 June 2026 Deadline: If Your Tenancy Agreement Was Verbal, You Face a £7,000 Fine for Missing Today's Cutoff

Published 1 June 2026

Key Takeaways
  • Landlords with wholly oral tenancies had until 1 June 2026 to issue a written statement of terms — or face a fine of up to £7,000 The obligation comes from Schedule 6, paragraph 7(5) of the Renters' Rights Act 2025, with content requirements set by SI 2026/324 The written statement must cover 17 specific items — issuing the standard Information Sheet is not enough for oral tenancies If the deadline has passed, issue the statement immediately — ongoing breach can trigger a further penalty after 28 days This fine is not covered by landlord insurance. You pay it personally.
  • If you have a tenant on a verbal agreement and you have not put it in writing, your deadline was 1 June 2026. Miss it, and your local council can fine you up to £7,000. This is not a planning requirement or a licensing condition. It is a new legal duty under the Renters' Rights Act 2025, backed by secondary legislation that came into force on 1 May 2026. Here is exactly what the law says, where to find it, and what you need to do right now.

What this means for landlords

Oral tenancy agreements have always been legally valid. What has changed is that the Renters' Rights Act 2025 now requires landlords to reduce them to writing — and for agreements that were already running before 1 May 2026, Parliament set a one-month grace period to do so. That period expired on 1 June 2026.

If you have not yet acted, the breach is already live. Acting now does not erase it, but it stops it from getting worse. A continuing breach can attract a further penalty.

What the law actually says

The primary legislation: Renters' Rights Act 2025, Schedule 6, paragraph 7(5)

The obligation for oral tenancies does not sit in the main body of the Act. It sits in the transitional provisions — specifically Schedule 6, paragraph 7(5) of the Renters' Rights Act 2025.

"Where an existing tenancy is wholly oral, section 16D(4) of the 1988 Act (inserted by section 12) is to be read as if, for 'before the tenancy is entered into' there were substituted 'before the end of the period of one month beginning with the commencement date (within the meaning given by section 146 of the Renters' Rights Act 2025)'." -- Renters' Rights Act 2025, Schedule 6, paragraph 7(5)

In plain English: the general rule under section 16D of the Housing Act 1988 is that a written statement must be given before a tenancy starts. For tenancies already running as oral agreements before 1 May 2026, the law swaps that requirement for a one-month deadline from commencement. The commencement date was 1 May 2026. The deadline was therefore 1 June 2026.

The secondary legislation: SI 2026/324

Section 16D by itself only creates the duty to provide a written statement. It does not say what must go in it. That detail is set out in The Assured Tenancies (Private Rented Sector) (Written Statement of Terms etc and Information Sheet) (England) Regulations 2026 (SI 2026 No. 324), made on 18 March 2026 and in force from 1 May 2026.

Regulation 2 of SI 2026/324 provides that the written statement must include the terms and information set out in the Schedule to those Regulations. This applies explicitly to wholly oral tenancies entered into before 1 May 2026.

Section summary
  • The duty to issue a written statement comes from Schedule 6, paragraph 7(5) of the Renters' Rights Act 2025 The content of that statement is prescribed by SI 2026/324 — you cannot decide what to include The deadline was 1 June 2026. If you have not acted, the breach is already running.

What must go in the written statement

The Schedule to SI 2026/324 sets out 17 items. Every written statement for an oral tenancy must cover all that apply. This is not a checklist you can cherry-pick from — if an item applies to the tenancy, it must be included.

Oral tenancy vs written tenancy — two different obligations

Why this matters

Many landlords have received guidance telling them to issue the Renters' Rights Act Information Sheet to their existing tenants. That is correct — but only for tenancies that were already wholly or partly in writing. The obligation for oral tenancies goes further.

For existing written tenancies, Regulation 3 of SI 2026/324 specifies that the information to be given is the Renters' Rights Act Information Sheet 2026, produced by the Secretary of State. It is available at: https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/the-renters-rights-act-information-sheet-2026

That document does not satisfy the obligation for a wholly oral tenancy — you need the full written statement of terms.

Questions landlords ask about this

Q: Does issuing the Information Sheet cover me if my tenancy was always verbal? A: No. The Information Sheet satisfies the obligation under paragraph 7(2) of Schedule 6 to the Renters' Rights Act 2025, which applies to existing written tenancies. For a wholly oral tenancy, you need a full written statement of terms covering all 17 items in the Schedule to SI 2026/324. The two obligations are separate and the Information Sheet does not substitute for the written statement.

Q: What if I use a managing agent — is it their responsibility? A: Section 16D(8) of the Housing Act 1988 (as inserted by the Renters' Rights Act 2025) provides that where a landlord has a contract requiring an agent to ensure compliance, the duty also applies to the agent. However, this does not remove your liability as landlord. If the agent has not acted, the council can still come after you. Chase your agent today and get written confirmation that the statement has been issued.

Q: If I miss the deadline, can I still issue the statement now? A: Yes — and you should, immediately. Issuing the statement late does not erase the breach, but it stops it from continuing. Under section 16I(3) of the Housing Act 1988 (as inserted by section 15 of the Renters' Rights Act 2025), a further financial penalty can be imposed if a breach continues for more than 28 days after a final notice. Getting the statement out now limits your exposure.

The penalty: up to £7,000

How enforcement works

The financial penalty is imposed under section 16I of the Housing Act 1988 (as inserted by section 15 of the Renters' Rights Act 2025). The local housing authority — your London borough council — can impose a penalty if it is satisfied beyond reasonable doubt that you have failed to comply with section 16D.

The maximum single penalty is £7,000. If the breach continues for more than 28 days after a final notice is issued, a further penalty can be imposed. Penalties are joint and several where more than one person — for example, a landlord and their agent — is responsible for the same breach.

The process runs: notice of intent → 28-day period for representations → final notice → 28 days to pay. You can appeal to the First-tier Tribunal (Property Chamber) within 28 days of the final notice.

A local authority can issue a fine of up to £7,000 without going to court — no criminal conviction required. If the breach continues beyond 28 days after a final notice, a further penalty can be imposed on top of the first.

Coming soon

The following provisions are in the Renters' Rights Act 2025 but have not yet commenced as of 1 June 2026.

The Private Rented Sector Database (Part 3 of the Act) will require landlords to register properties before letting them. Once in force, failure to register will be a separate offence carrying a financial penalty of up to £7,000, rising to a criminal offence on repeat breach. No commencement date has been confirmed.

The Private Rented Sector Ombudsman (Part 4 of the Act) will require all private landlords in England to join a redress scheme. Failure to do so will also carry financial penalties. No commencement date has been confirmed.

Does your insurance cover this?

No. Legal expenses cover in landlord policies typically covers disputes with tenants — rent arrears, possession proceedings, property damage claims. It does not cover regulatory fines imposed by a local authority for breach of statutory duties.

Some policies include cover for defence costs if you are investigated or prosecuted, but the fine itself is not covered. If you are unsure, read your policy schedule under 'what is not covered' and look specifically for exclusions relating to fines, penalties, or statutory obligations. Do not assume cover exists.

What to do right now

These steps apply to current obligations only.

  • Identify every tenancy you hold where there is no written agreement.
  • Prepare a written statement covering all 17 items in the Schedule to SI 2026/324. The full list is set out in this article above.
  • Include in the statement any prior-notice possession grounds you may want to rely on in future — particularly Ground 1 (owner occupation), Ground 1A (sale), or any student lettings grounds.
  • Send the written statement to the tenant. Email with a read receipt or delivery confirmation is fine — keep a copy of what you sent and when.
  • For any existing tenancy that was already in writing, separately ensure you have sent the Renters' Rights Act Information Sheet 2026, available at: https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/the-renters-rights-act-information-sheet-2026
  • If you use a managing agent, confirm in writing today that they have complied with both obligations for your portfolio. Do not assume.

The bottom line

The 1 June 2026 deadline for oral tenancy agreements is not guidance — it is statutory, grounded in Schedule 6, paragraph 7(5) of the Renters' Rights Act 2025 and given teeth by section 16I of the Housing Act 1988 as inserted by section 15 of the Renters' Rights Act 2025. The maximum fine is £7,000, your insurer will not pay it, and the penalty can be repeated if the breach continues.

The required content of the written statement is set out in the Schedule to SI 2026/324. It is not onerous — 17 items, most of which you can complete in an hour. The risk of not doing it is out of all proportion to the effort of doing it.

If your tenancy was verbal and you have not yet issued a written statement, send it today.
